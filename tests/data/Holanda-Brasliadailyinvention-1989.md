# Brasília: The daily invention of the city

## Frederico de Holanda

The author, an architect with studies in Brazi! and the U.K. — MSc Course in Advanced Architectural Studies at University College London — and with experience in building projects, university campus studies, and urban design of small Brazilian cities, is at present Director of the Institute of Architecture and Town Planning at the University of Brasília, Brazil.

## Introduction

Brasília is known internationally as one of the largestscale experiments realized.under the doctrine of the Modern Movement in Architecture and Urbanism. However, the city as it presently exists by far transcends the purity of the original idea, even though it strongly keeps its birthmark. Rather than a single idea about urban morphology, we have witnessed, from the very start, conflicting ways in terms of conceiving, producing and using the urban space, as well as in the process of implementing the plan over the landscape.

I call these ways spatial codes and argue that they constitute powerful means of social and cultural identification.1 The different spatial codes have either appeared simultaneously in different parts of the urban structure, or co-existed in parts of the urban structure and “struggled" over time against each other. Depending on the power that determinate groups of social agents have had occasionally over the production and use of such places, one or the other spatial code has eventually dominated.

This text is about the different ways of structuring city-form and city-use as manifested in the capital city of Brazil, and also about the social implications that go along with these. The reader will not find here an extensive survey of the physical morphologies of the Federal District.² Rather the paper will bring to the fore some examples only to illustrate the main point of this argument, namely that: even in situations of very strong control over the production and form of urban space by central planning agencies, the prevention of the unfolding of opposing codes is impossible.

The paper deals with three examples (fig. 1):

• the 405/6 North Super-Block;

• the Paranoá settlement; and,

• the Road Platform.

In each of the first two examples — which are both residential areas — we find codes which have become stable over the years and which closely correspond to the different modes of life unfolding in these areas. The third example, the Road Platform, is one among a number of instances that may illustrate “change in the use of

## The 405/6 North Super-Block

The area under study (405/6 NSB) was one of the first built in the North Wing of the Pilot Plan of Brasília (fig. 2). Although the area is still inhabited by a population of middle and middle-low income, and about 34 percent of households have lived here for ten years or more — which suggests reasonably consolidated spatial habits — in recent years there began a clear process of gentrification.

Morphological attributes: In the area delimited for measuring — which covers 237,552 sq.m:

• The ratio between closed and open space is very low even if compared with other fractions in Brasília itself (fig. 3): only 8.6 percent of the total area is closed space.

• The mean convex space (here defined as a piece of the open space system, every point in the plan of which can be seen from every other point) is 1,446 sq.m (fig. 4).

• The pilotis in all residential buildings reduce to a minimum the occupation of the ground level: spatial islands (i.e. the continuous bits of closed space) coincide only with staircases (fig. 5), rooms used by doormen and their families and, more recently, rooms built for parties or other meetings of the people who live in each building.

• These very small islands reduce the relation between “perimeter” and “constitution"(building entrances), i.e. the apertures between closed and open space, to only 23 linear meters per building entrance. However, because of the very low occupation ratio of the overall ground surface, we have 1,142 sq.m of open space per building entrance.

• Blind spaces, i.e. convex spaces with no doors opening onto them, are in great number: 67 percent of the total of convex spaces have no building entrances — a fact that brings the Super-Block very close even to the most ceremonial spaces in Brasília, such as the Esplanade of Ministries.

• Except for the shops at the local shop sector, there is no aggregation of “primary cells," if by this we understand the individual buildings constituting the settlement.

• Spatial islands coinciding with the staircases, or with isolated buildings such as the school, the church, the gas-station and the newsstand (figs. 6 and 7), contribute strongly to the high percentage of blind spaces.

• The mean number of building entrances per convex space becomes large — 1, 28 — because of the contribution of the local shopping.

• The axial map — constructed through the insertion of the smallest number of the longest straight lines on the plan — reveals the most important morphological feature of the area under study: not a single line is deeper than 1 from the “outer space"of the fraction (fig. 8).

• Relative asymmetry, which gives us the degree to which the system of lines is more or less integrated,⁵ is thus 0, in respect of the exterior space of the fraction, which means that the Super-Block is the shallowest solution possible concerning its relationship of permeability with the space that surrounds it.

Standard deviation is also very low indeed (0.0983), compared, for example, with that of Paranoá (0.1897).

• Grid axiality — a measure that reveals whether or not a morphology approximates a perfect orthogonal grid, ranging from 0 (maximum deformation) to 1 (minimum deformation) — amounts to 0.4630, very far from that of Paranoá.

• The axial map is somehow arbitrary, in the sense that many other different axial maps might be drawn, with lines in many different directions, due to the extreme permeability of the place (contrary to the case of Paranoá where axial structure is extremely "deterministic,"i.e. the map drawn is practically the only one possible to be drawn).

Two observations should be made concerning this last point:

• First, there is a strong correlation between this syntactic undifferentiation and the clearly noticeable even distribution of people throughout the place. Such lack of differentiation — and the corresponding lack of structure in the distribution of use — may be responsible for the low legibility of spaces in the Pilot Plan, so often commented on⁶: generally, places do not differ amongst themselves according to size, integration/segregation or constitution of spaces, but according to geometrical form, color or texture of individual buildings. Order thus arises only from secondary or accessory elements of the spatial system.

Social attributes: An exploratory inquiry made in the 405/6 NSB through 44 home interviews,7 the objective of which was to learn about the social practices of the inhabitants in relation to their urban context and about their assessment of the Super-Block and the city at large, reveals the following:

● The inhabitants are basically middle class civil servants, the majority holding a university degree and owning the flat in which they live.

• The means of transportation mostly used is the bus, but the private car comes close as a second option.

• In their network of encounters in leisure time, first and foremost come the relationships with relatives and secondly with neighbors.

• Social contacts take place mainly in the domestic space (one's own, relatives' or friends'), which has clear implications as to the mode of appropriation of the city.

Asked to point out positive and negative aspects of their immediate place of living, i.e. the Super-Block, the inhabitants reacted with a positive overall assessment. When asked about what they liked most, they pinpointed the availability of open spaces (they were always concerned with children), the availability of goods and services, security as an intrinsic aspect of living in flats, and the sanitary quality of the place — which are all aspects not of a morphological kind (except the relationship of the flats with the ground level).

The opposite holds with the negative aspects commented on by those who were critical and who pointed out the following problems: lack of desirable relationships between internal and external spaces which would make contact between “passer by" and inhabitant easier, and would work as an incentive towards casual promenades on the sidewalks; rare contacts with neighbors, because of the non-existence of spatial devices such as dividing walls of plots, front gardens or the street itself; difficult mutual support amongst families in the supervision of children; impossibility of expanding the housing unit, in order to cope with emerging family needs;

impossibility of having a garden; scarce movement of people in the open spaces, even if such movement was just to be seen from the window; scarce use of open space, even at weekends or for traditional Brazilian street parties, such as the holiday of St. John in June.

For the majority of people those aspects they indicated as positive weighed more than the negative ones. However, in this, as in any other case, advantages or disadvantages of determinate morphological attributes are intimately related to the insertion of the inhabitants in specific modes of life (urban or otherwise). The majority of people build their network of relationships within family ties outside their neighborhood, and have a relatively easy mobility in the city. To them, it is clear that morphological attributes which provide easier neighborhood encounters would not matter decisively in their assessment of the place. This suggests that a critique of the Pilot Plan, as well as of the basic principles of the Modern Movement morphology as manichaeistically “bad" for all, must be considered carefully. Class condition must not be underweighed. It is a plausible hypothesis that the majority of those who live in the Pilot Plan are not “distressed"by the absence of street corners nor do they suffer the so-called “plateau syndrome" — a usual expression which conveys the state of mind of those who miss the urban life they left behind in cities like Rio de Janeiro or São Paulo. Rather, the middle class inhabitants, by their very class condition, approve of these places. Their “needs" in regard to open spaces have to do with areas for the children and distances between buildings which allow good conditions of air and light, both amply satisfied here. They have nothing to do with the system of open spaces as a constituting element of a system of encounters, for their behavior is essentially transpatial⁸ andindoors-biased.

Further research will eventually show that the more one climbs the steps of the social pyramid, the more this will prove to be the case.

The opposite holds in the other direction as shown by the analysis of Paranoá, one of the poorest neighborhoods of the Federal District urban system.

## Paranoá

The Paranoá “invasion" — a local euphemism for slum — developed close to the site of one of the first construction camps in Brasília, the one established for works involved in building the dam which would form the lake embracing the Pilot Plan.

Across the lake, ironically situated just in front of the Palace of the Dawn, the official residence of the President of the Republic, the settlement may be reached by road, 28 km away from the Bus Station at the Road Platform, located at the crossing of the monumental axis and the road axis of the Pilot Plan (fig. 1). The place (fig. 9) has been built and is continuing to be built by the people themselves, without interference of any planning agency in its design (figs. 10-14). It now reaches about 35,000 people of the lowest income in the Federal District, half of them at least earning only up to two “minimum wages" (i.e. the minimum amount of money a worker should earn in Brazil, which is equivalent today to about US \$ 60.00 a month).9

The majority of employed men work in the building industry. Àt the time of our inquiry unemployment was about 30 percent of adult men, while about 84 percent of women worked at home.

Attributes of the physical morpholgy: For analytic measuring, an area of 412,240 sq.m has been delimited which corresponds to almost a third of the total area of the settlement.

● The mean convex space is 410.25 sq.m (fig. 15).

Open space amounts to 38.2 percent of the total ground surface, in great contrast with the Super-Block. It is intensely constituted: 2.96 building entrances per convex space, 138.79 sq.m of open space per building entrance and 19.3 linear meters of island perimeter per building entrance. Despite this, we still find 17.4 percent of blind spaces (which is very much related to the irregular geometry of the blocks); but even so, this is a ratio quite distinct from any fraction of the Pilot Plan.

● The axial order of Paranoá (fig. 16) is as well radically different from the Super-Block.

— Firstly, grid axiality is only 0.114, thus very far from that of a perfect grid.

— Secondly, local control of places is much more evident, through the many lines 4 or 5 steps deep from the carrier.

— Thirdly, mean depth of lines ranges from a minimum of 3.4922 to a maximum of 8.5855, the mean depth in the system as a whole being 5.4044.

— Still, Relative Asymmetry and Real Relative Asymmetry are low, the means for the system being respectively 0.0459 and 0.8656, which shows a well integrated system.

● Of special importance is the hierarchy of axes which order the place and differentiate spaces among themselves. The 10 percent most integrated lines (fig. 17) roughly form a semi-wheel with a hub and 4 to 5 spokes. In the hub. as it were, concentrate 11 of the 19 most integrated lines and here is where the main establishments for the daily shopping and the weekly fair take place.

● The spokes penetrate the place in 3 main directions, one of them bifurcating in two sub-directions. This marks global control areas,1º along the axis, and local control areas in between, contrasting with the Super-Block where only global control exists. Also, if one superimposes integration and control maps (fig. 18), one notices that 6 of the 19 most integrated lines also belong to the set of the top control lines of the settlement. Control (a local dimension) is thus invested in strongly integrated lines (a global dimension), and this seems to favor the interface between inhabitant and stranger in Paranoá. This has been checked through the localization of central activities: 66.7 percent of the total number of establishments in the study area are situated along the 10 percent top integrated lines, and 46.1 percent are situated in lines which belong to both sets (figs. 19 and 20). There exists a sort of convergence of local and global orders, through syntactic investment in six main axes of the whole set.

Social attributes: Almost needless to add, it is in these spaces that people concentrate for various mobile or static leisure activities. Such condensation of life also contrasts Paranoá with fractions of the Pilot Plan, mainly for the fact that, in the former, this is intimately connected with the spatial morphology itself. It is not chance or accessory factors — such as, for instance, a better treated piece of green area at the 405/6 NSB, or still the peculiar nature of an important land use — which originate greater appropriation of people in particular places, but the qualifications of the spatial structure.

## The Road Platform

The Road Platform is a huge and complex building and, for all its problems and potentialities, it is perhaps one of the most fascinating bits of the Pilot Plan of Brasília (fig. 1, no. 1). It is a four-level structure (underground vehicle passage, ground level, mezzanine and a higher parking and circulation deck for cars and pedestrians), which connects the two residential wings and crosses at ground level with the monumental axis (figs. 21, 22 and 23).

The important issue here to deal with in the case of the Road Platform is the transformations in use and the measures that the Government has taken in order to cope with these transformations, considering the place as a whole.

The Road Platform is the place in the urban center of Brasília where the presence of people is most dense, varied, continuous and prolonged. It concentrates the urban terminals of buses which run in the Pilot Plan and which connect the plan to the satellite nuclei. The Road Platform could rightly be characterized as a “public transport sector," although it is not named thus; and because of its very function, one finds here the most diverse people, who come to the center for the most diverse reasons.

Categories are more superimposed here than anywhere else in the settlement. It has not been by pure chance that human behavior and activity in the area soon superseded those more directly related with its function, transforming the space into one of the most lively meeting places of the capital. No doubt, as a solution it is a curiously contradictory one. On the one hand the logic of separation demands the concentration of bus terminals in one place, taking away from other areas of the center a factor which always contributes to its appropriation. On the other, mono-functionality here is distinct, because its effects on space do not have the temporary and categorical peculiarities which obtain in other sectors. The clerk, the civil servant, the student, the unemployed looking for a job, all “metamorphose"themselves into “passengers" (this is the “label" they assume here), but nevertheless they keep their characteristics and the effect they have on the spaces they occupy, which constitute their mode of using and making the settlement.

What happened to the Road Platform in 1981 illustrates the logic that presides over the spatial organization of this social universe or, as Hillier and Leaman put it,13 the “pre-structures of design"with which planners work. As the clarity, lack of ambiguity, and pre-determination implicit in the morphology of correspondence were quickly disappearing from the place, it was considered urgent to rescue the“order"thus jeopardized. The “solution" which ought to spring from such reading would consist in re-separating the categories found here, withln a new framework. Let us see how.

Until 1981, the Road Platform housed the urban and the inter-urban bus terminals, i.e. those which connect Brasília to other cities or state capitals. The distant traveller, on arrival in Brasília, thus reached the very core of the city. This type of “agent" had a specific contribution to the appropriation of the space, not only because of the sheer number of people, but because of the specific demands they made on the place, where they stayed for longer periods and used services more frequently than the capital dweller. “Overcrowding" was then identified.

The diagnosis is in itself irrelevant for our analysis. What matters in fact is the “solution" given to the "problem"; the solution consisted of the removal of the inter-urban buses to a new and distant terminal, located at a place reserved for a future railway station, which then became the “Road-Railway Station" of Brasília, located at the west end of the monumental axis (fig. 1).

With this action an intensely used place in the center was emptied and, at the same time, one more of these typical poles of the morphology of separation was created, i.e. a place at once specialized and radically isolated from any occupied neighborhood.

As for the Road Platform, the “pre-structures of design" would not allow alternative solutions, such as,

— the permanence of inter-urban terminals in the Center and the gradual transference of urban terminals to other points of the same central area, as long as the Road Platform in fact showed overcrowded; or,

— the expansion of the Road Platform, over the free spaces which surround and isolate it from neighboring sectors; because in both alternatives the categorical “purity” would be lost, as far as these secondary terminals (which exist elsewhere) would have significant effects on the appropriation of these other sites.

This mode of expansion, typical of this morphology — which unfolds not through densification of existing parts of the spatial fabric, nor through aggregation of new areas immediately adjacent to existing ones, but through the multiplication of isolated “poles" — illustrates, in a localized event, the same logic which gave birth to the satellite nuclei in Brasília. These satellite nuclei have not been created because of an overcrowded Pilot Plan — which is still today about 1/5 empty — but in order to house the lower income people and to keep the Pilot Plan as a clearly identifiable spatial unit. The satellite nuclei are isolated settlements themselves, linked nevertheless in daily life with the “mother city," through a large number of trips from home to work or services and vice versa.

Such “rescue" of the morphological order takes place through state macro-interventions which, in a short period of time, radically modify a mode of appropriation which has been consolidated through years. In a place like Brasília, centralized control of the making of the space and of its usage reaches extreme limits, but it is not yet a "total institution," in Goffman's terms,4. A large part of spatial control — on the making or on the use of space — is still distributed amongst the people, the inhabitants who through various devices permanently re-orient the spatial system towards directions unsuspected by central planning. When such re-orientations seriously jeopardize the dominant morphological principles, central intervention comes to the fore and reestablishes morphological“order."

## Conclusion

The three areas of the Federal District in Brazil discussed in this paper — two residential fractions and one central area — illustrate not only the congruence between modes of life of different social classes and diverse urban morphologies, but also the tension which is eventually identifiable in some places, along time, between different principles of spatial order.

The 405/6 North Super-Block is a residential instance of morphological principles that are found in the rest of the Pilot Plan and in many satellite nuclei as well, while Paranoá is an instance of a subordinate mode of spatial structuration, which is synchronically produced and reproduced in the interstices of the dominant morphology of a contemporary complex society.

In the case of the Super-Block, of special importance is not simply the amount of green area (although this also matters) but mainly its morphology and more specifically the fact that:

— direct transitions between internal and external space disappear as, on the ground, only the closed transition spaces of the staircases exist; and,

— these primary cells, isolated among themselves, do not define a physical neighborhood, as found in Paranoá where the real concept of “neighborhood" was replaced by the idea of the “neighborhood unit," again an abstract, conceptual space.

Furthermore, if the notion of “neighborhood" implies some sort of local control of space by the inhabitants, thus pointing to a strengthening of “community links,"the radical absence of building entrances from such places and their radical openness or exposure in relation to the outer space denies the intention on the discourse. This absolute openness both eliminates local control and dissolves the presence of strangers, who disperse throughout space, without constituting any denser interface with the inhabitant, as again clearly occurs in Paranoá. The concrete activities observed in Paranoá, which take place in the open spaces and which depend on an intimate relation between indoors and outdoors, warn us against the belief that spaces like those of the Super-Block are universally suitable for people, because such universalization is only viable if we accept a "naturalization" of people's “needs," which is obviously not the case.

On the contrary, the level of satisfaction of the inhabitants with the space of the Super-Block goes hand in hand with an indoors-biased mode of life which dispenses with physical propinquity altogether. The opposite holds in Paranoá: high constitution, local control, intense interface among inhabitants of the settlement and between inhabitants and strangers, seems fundamental for the production and reproduction of a mode of life which in large measure depends on a rich and intense system of encounters based on the open spaces of the settlement.

But such a system of encounters refuses to remain a simple isolated eccentricity and attacks the very core of the dominant morphology. The transformation in use of a strategic point — the Road Platform — and the reaction that such transformation caused on the part of the governmental planning agency, exemplify a permanent tension between different principles of spatial order which sprawls out, in greater or lesser degree, all over the place.

The daily invention of the city incorporates such a tension.
